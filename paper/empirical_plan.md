# Empirical work plan — what to do before expanding the paper

Following Mani's feedback on draft_v0, this document lays out the
empirical work needed before the paper is worth writing further. The
work is illustrative, not quantitative. Goal: have enough concrete
material to make the contribution credible to a reader.

---

## Three pieces of work

The empirical contribution rests on three pieces. Each can be done
independently; together they make the paper.

### Piece A — The office mental model, documented

**Purpose.** Claim 2 (office mental model) is new and needs to be
expressed clearly enough that a reader sees it as a contribution.
Without explicit documentation, the metaphor is implicit in our
substrate but does not surface as a contribution.

**Deliverable.** A short document — maybe 3 to 5 pages — titled
something like "Pat's office: a workplace metaphor for distributed
sense-and-respond systems." It explains the mental model in everyday
vocabulary:

- The system is an office; the office has a name.
- The office has *employees* (agents). Each employee has a *job
  description* in English (the role.md file).
- Employees pass *memos* to each other (messages). The *org chart*
  (office.md) shows who passes memos to whom.
- The office has *mailrooms / front desks* (sources) that bring work
  in from the world (RSS feeds, microphones, gmail, sensors).
- The office has *outbound services* (sinks) that take finished work
  out (display, JSONL files, Slack alerts).
- An employee might have a *router-style decision*: depending on what
  they read, they pass the memo to a different colleague.
- An employee can also pass the memo *back* to an earlier colleague —
  for a *revise and resubmit* pattern (feedback loop).

Each concept gets one or two paragraphs in the document, with a
small example.

The document is the central pedagogical artifact of the paper. It
introduces Pat to the system; it also introduces the reader of the
paper to what we mean by "office mental model."

**Estimate.** One focused day to write.

### Piece B — Case studies of iterative development

**Purpose.** Claim 3 (iteration at the English level) needs concrete
illustration. The reader has to see what happens when a Pat-like
person works with Claude over multiple rounds.

**Deliverable.** Two or three case studies. Each one a self-contained
document that includes:

- The starting English description Pat gave (informal, the way a
  non-programmer would write it).
- Iteration log: each round is a back-and-forth.
  - Round 0: what Pat originally asked for.
  - Round 1: what Claude built. Office.md, role files, brief
    description of the Python.
  - Round 2: what Pat then said. ("Change the display." "Also send
    to Slack." "The classifier should also look at the headline.")
    What Claude did.
  - Etc.
  - Final round: the office Pat accepted.
- Brief commentary: what was easy, what required clarification, what
  was unexpected.

The case studies are illustrative — they tell the reader "here is
what working with Claude on an S&R office actually looks like."

Recommended cases to develop:

| # | App | Pattern shown | Why this one |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | situation_room | Pipeline + router | Canonical example; we already have full artifacts |
| 2 | loudness_monitor | Pipeline with Python agents (numeric, librosa/numpy) | Tests the "Pat doesn't need Python" claim — agents that wrap numpy/etc are generated by Claude |
| 3 | inbox_triage | Three-way router | A different topology; simple Pat-facing description |

A fourth, if time allows, would be a feedback case (a small debate
or solver-with-critic). The 04_debate sanity case we already have
could be adapted.

**Estimate.** Each case study is roughly one focused day if Claude is
playing both the dev role (responding to Pat) and the simulated Pat
(making the iteration requests). Two to three days total for the
three cases.

If a real Pat-like person could sit with Claude for one or two of
these sessions, the case studies become much more convincing. That
is not necessary for the paper but it would help.

### Piece C — Architecture and substrate description

**Purpose.** Claim 1 (two-layer) and Claim 4 (substrate suffices)
both rest on the architecture. The reader has to see how the pieces
fit together.

**Deliverable.** A short architecture document with three sections:

1. **The substrate (DSL).** What it provides:
   - Agent / message abstraction.
   - Message routing through office.md connections.
   - Per-agent state.
   - Snapshot and resume.
   - Source and sink registries.

2. **The English specification layer.** What it consists of:
   - office.md format (grammar, examples, parser).
   - role.md format (frontmatter, English contract, JSON output
     discipline, port declaration).

3. **The Python implementation layer and how the LLM maintains it.**
   - For LLM agents, the role.md prompt body is the implementation;
     the runtime executes it directly.
   - For Python agents (e.g., numeric/audio), Claude generates the
     Python from the English contract and wraps it via the agent
     contract.
   - How modifications propagate: Pat edits English, the LLM
     regenerates Python if needed, the substrate executes.

This document mostly exists already (in DECISIONS.md, in the
translation table, in the README). The work is consolidating and
cleaning, not creating.

**Estimate.** One focused day.

## Suggested order

I would do them in this order:

1. **Piece A first.** Writing the office mental model is the
   smallest and cleanest piece. It also forces us to commit to the
   vocabulary we use throughout the paper.

2. **Piece C second.** Once the vocabulary is fixed, the architecture
   document follows easily.

3. **Piece B last.** The case studies are written using the
   vocabulary and the architecture from A and C. Writing them last
   means we are not iterating vocabulary while running case studies.

Total estimate: roughly one week of focused work.

---

## What we should NOT do

A few things tempted me as I planned and I want to be explicit about
not doing them:

- **No user study.** We do not need to recruit users to run
  iteration sessions on. Claude playing both sides is sufficient for
  illustrative case studies. A real user study would be a different
  paper.

- **No quantitative measurement.** No rounds-to-convergence, no
  reuse rates, no library-coverage statistics. These would all be
  honest but they are not what the paper is claiming.

- **No comparison with baselines.** No "NoT vs direct LLM Python"
  comparison. We have already conceded that the LLM can do this
  directly; we are arguing for the *form* of the artifact, not the
  *capability* of producing one.

- **No expansion of the Phase 2 / Phase 3 plan.** Multi-flow,
  Python-vertex orchestration, full gallery regeneration — all
  deferred. The paper does not need them.
